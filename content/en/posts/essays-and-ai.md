Today I want to share a personal take on that strange trend that seems to push AI CEOs to write long essays. I'm writing this following the essay by Meta's CEO (https://www.meta.com/thefutureisforeveryone/), which accompanies Meta's return as a leader among American open weight model companies.

This essay is a bit of Mark Zuckerberg's answer to Dario Amodei, the first and original writer of long essays.
I find these essays interesting, because these people, who are at the top of the leading AI companies, dedicate (in my opinion) a considerable amount of time to writing them.

And why do they do it? It seems that controlling the AI narrative is very important for a company that does AI.
This is because two things seem to follow: the moves of the US legislator and the response of the best AI talent.

Setting yourself up as a champion of the public good wins over new talent, as happened with Anthropic.
It's also an opportunity to tell the legislator how to write the rules everyone will have to follow.
It can also be a way to influence competitor companies or push competitors to respond.

Still, the contrast between Anthropic's line and Meta's is clear.
Anthropic has defined itself as the guardian of advanced AI, while Meta wants to distribute it to everyone.
So far Meta has promised to release Muse Spark 1.2, a near-frontier model; it would certainly be the most advanced American open weight model.

So it seems that this debate is being won by the open weight world, now with American support for Chinese models.

Until recently the OpenAI-Anthropic duopoly was seen as the true leader, but today I'd say they could only hold this leadership for short periods, before a rival company releases equivalent models.

It's two companies against 5: Meta, Moonshot AI, DeepSeek, Qwen, GLM.

These leading companies are still doing well because they are still seen as leaders, but one day, once the public is convinced that is no longer the case, they'll find a very competitive market, with fierce rivals.
